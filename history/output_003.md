The name “Mother Teresa” carries the weight of sainthood. To many, she is a symbol of compassion, a woman who dedicated her life to the poorest of the poor, a figure whose global reverence earned her the Nobel Peace Prize in 1979. Yet beneath the halo lies a story that defies the neatness of a saintly narrative. The Missionaries of Charity, the organization she founded, has been implicated in practices so starkly at odds with its humanitarian mission that they raise urgent questions about intention, context, and the cost of charity. This is not a story of outright malice, but of contradictions so profound they fracture the very image of selfless service she cultivated.

Let us begin with the secret baptisms. In India, where religion often shapes identity and inheritance, the act of religious conversion can feel like a violent intrusion. For decades, reports emerged that Mother Teresa’s missionaries would administer unannounced baptisms to dying Hindus and Muslims, sometimes without their families’ knowledge. The practice, though not universally confirmed, was well enough documented to draw condemnation from Indian lawmakers. One Hindu leader described it as a “spiritual theft,” a means of exploiting vulnerability to reshape lives under the guise of mercy. The logic, as explained by some within the Church, was pastoral: saving a soul was more urgent than respecting familial wishes. But to families left grieving a loved one who had been rebaptized against their will, the act felt like a final violation.

This tension between spiritual conviction and ethical boundaries extends to the clinics operated by the Missionaries of Charity. While these centers, often located in the filth-choked streets of Kolkata, provided critical care to those with no other options, investigations in the 1990s revealed alarming oversights. Painkillers for cancer patients were regularly denied, a practice tied to Catholic doctrine that views euthanasia as gravely immoral. Morphine, a drug that could ease suffering, was sometimes withheld under the assumption that prolonging life—even in agony—was the only moral course. The result? Patients, some barely conscious, would lie in excruciating pain, their bodies twitching as their minds faded. One nurse on staff later described the scene as “a punishment for the poor,” a system where faith overrode medicine.

The reuse of unsterilized needles in these same clinics compounds the horror. In a country with limited healthcare infrastructure, basic sanitation is often the difference between infection and survival. Yet in Mother Teresa’s clinics, needles were reportedly reused without proper cleaning, a practice that, in theory, could have spread HIV and other bloodborne pathogens. This was not the result of underfunding but of systemic neglect. A 1996 report by *The Hindu* newspaper cited staff who claimed needles were simply “rinsed and reused” due to cost constraints. Critics argue this reflects a broader failure to balance compassion with competence, a failure that turned places of mercy into sites of preventable harm.

Meanwhile, the Missionaries of Charity’s orphanages operated under a different kind of ethical fog. Adoption laws in India are complex, designed to protect children from exploitation. Yet staff at some of Mother Teresa’s institutions were accused of delaying or blocking adoptions, keeping children in overcrowded, understaffed facilities for years. The stated reason? Preference for placing children with Catholic families. In practice, this meant prolonged institutional care for children who might otherwise have found homes. Interviews with former residents paint a picture of neglect: children with severe illnesses left unattended, staff who spoke little to them, and a pervasive sense of being “seen, but never heard.”

The controversies deepen when we consider Mother Teresa’s relationships. She corresponded with figures like Indira Gandhi, whose political ruthlessness was matched only by her ambition, and with Pope John Paul II, whose papacy has since been scrutinized for handling of child abuse cases. There is also the matter of her ties to Carlos the Jackal, the Marxist terrorist, and to Charles Keating, a fraudster who orchestrated a billion-dollar investment scheme. These connections, tenuous as they may seem, suggest a willingness to navigate morally ambiguous spaces for the sake of influence or resources.

The most difficult question remains: what did Mother Teresa herself know about these practices? Supporters often insist that the horrors described here were the work of overzealous staff, not her direct design. Yet one cannot ignore the institutional culture she encouraged—hierarchies where dissent was discouraged, and where religious obedience was prioritized over transparency. A 2010 Vatican investigation into her canonization process revealed that some within the Church were uncomfortable with her legacy, citing the same controversies detailed here. The resulting sainthood, granted in 2016, was a political decision as much as a spiritual one.

To engage with this history is not to erase the real good done by the Missionaries of Charity, nor to dismiss the millions of lives touched by Mother Teresa’s work. It is to confront the uncomfortable truth that even the most venerated figures can embody contradictions. Charity, when entangled with dogma or institutional inertia, risks becoming a vehicle for harm as much as help. The clinics, orphanages, and hospices she established remain vital in many communities—yet their legacy is indelibly marked by the practices that shadowed them.

What stays with us, then, is not a saint but a mirror. Mother Teresa’s story reflects the seductive power of moral certainty and the fragility of accountability. It reminds us that compassion, when untempered by scrutiny, can become a tool of control. And it challenges us to ask, not just who we revere, but *why* we revere them.
